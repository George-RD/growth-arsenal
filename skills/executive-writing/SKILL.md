# Executive Writing

Turn a correct factual kernel into writing a manager can skim, hear and repeat.

Read `writing-core` first. This skill changes **delivery**, not the underlying facts.

## 1. Define the decision surface

State:

- who reads or hears it;
- what they already know;
- what decision, action or understanding is needed;
- whether the text must stand alone or accompanies a presenter.

*Done when:* the writing has one practical job.

## 2. Put the answer where the reader looks first

For a decision, lead with the recommendation or current conclusion. For an update, lead with what changed. For an exploratory session, lead with the question worth discussing.

Do not add an executive-sounding slogan in place of the answer.

*Done when:* the first one or two lines let the reader predict the rest.

## 3. Spend words on evidence and mechanism

Prefer:

- a number with a unit;
- a named operational example;
- a causal mechanism;
- a trade-off;
- a clear owner, date or next action.

Cut sentences whose main job is to sound strategic, balanced, reassuring or complete.

*Done when:* the reader can explain why the conclusion follows.

## 4. Match the surface

### Presentation

The slide is evidence for the speaker, not a transcript.

- Title: direct takeaway or question. Full sentence when natural; a short label is fine when the visual carries the claim.
- Body: the minimum evidence needed to understand or remember the point.
- Speaker notes: method, caveats, source detail and secondary arguments.
- Keep one main visual or calculation when it can replace prose.
- If the audience must read a paragraph, stop speaking until they have read it.

### Memo or report

Make the argument stand alone. Use short sections organised by reader questions, not decorative symmetry.

### Stakeholder update

Use outcome, current state, blocker or decision, then next action. Do not narrate all activity.

*Done when:* the information density fits how the surface is consumed.

## 5. Preserve natural corporate language

Use words a capable colleague would say in a meeting. Technical or commercial terms are fine when they are the shared language of the room.

Readability is a direction, not a universal threshold. Reduce unnecessary clause depth and formal vocabulary. Keep precise technical terms. For deterministic lint, use thresholds chosen for the audience rather than a single global grade target.

Common failure patterns:

- abstract nouns replacing actors and actions;
- symmetrical `X, not Y` conclusions;
- three-item lists created for rhythm;
- repeated status labels that turn every slide into a template;
- caveats pulled onto the slide because the writer is afraid to state a working estimate;
- headlines that describe the page instead of making the point.

*Done when:* the copy can be spoken without translating it back into ordinary English.

## 6. Final review

If the text is high-stakes or externally judged, run `business-copy-style` as the **final critic**. For internal technical/executive copy, use its structural review and an audience-appropriate readability threshold rather than forcing consumer-grade vocabulary.

A good executive draft is allowed to be plain, slightly uneven and specific. It does not need a polished takeaway on every section.
